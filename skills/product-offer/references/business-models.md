# Business models

The sixteen ways a product earns — *who pays, and for what relationship*. Read the "how the value arrives" table in full, then **only** the one or two models it points at. Pick one primary model; add a secondary only when the offer genuinely has two kinds of value (a base product plus paid add-ons). A usage overage on a subscription is still "subscription" — the overage belongs in [pricing-models.md](pricing-models.md). Every number below is an illustrative planning example, before tax.

## Start from how the value arrives

| The value is… | Start with |
|---|---|
| Continuous, recognised again at every renewal | Subscription |
| One bounded result the customer keeps | One-time purchase, Lifetime offer |
| Proportional to how much they consume | Usage-based, Credits |
| Extensions on a base product they already have | Paid content / add-ons |
| A completed exchange between two sides | Transaction / commission |
| Attention a third party pays to reach | Ads / sponsorship, Affiliate / lead generation |
| Bought by an organisation with procurement | Enterprise contracts, White-label / OEM |
| Free software, paid convenience or expertise | Open core / hosting, Software + services |
| A verified result | Outcome-based |
| Goodwill from a community | Donations / patronage |
| Indirect: it feeds another business the member runs | Supports another business |

**For a member productizing their skill**, three models come up most: software + services (the product plus the member's expertise), subscription with a capacity limit (the Designjoy pattern), and supports another business (a free tool or skill that feeds the member's paid service).

Each model below: when it fits, what tends to fail (quote this line to the member), a worked example, and a first test.

## 1. Subscription

- **Fits:** fitness and learning programmes, tools used every week, monitoring, team workflows. Weak for a task done once and never repeated.
- **Fails:** discounts postpone cancellation without fixing low value; features, notifications and annual lock-in don't create retention; expensive unlimited AI on a low fixed fee.
- **Example:** 100 customers × $20/mo = $2,000 MRR; at $4 direct cost each, $1,600 before acquisition and overhead. Losing 8 customers is $160/mo gone unless replaced.
- **First test:** ask "what will still be worth paying for in month six?" — then test a value-summary email against retained use, not a discount.

## 2. One-time purchase

- **Fits:** desktop utilities, offline tools, calculators, templates, bounded transformations — buyers tired of subscriptions, low ongoing cost.
- **Fails:** a price too low to fund acquisition or support; permanent hosting promised at today's server cost; "pay once" hiding a second required purchase.
- **Example:** $79 − $5 payment/refunds − $14 support reserve − $20 acquisition = $40 before development and overhead.
- **First test:** compare a $19 generic version with a $79 version for an expensive specialist task, and ask what evidence makes the $79 credible.

## 3. Usage-based

- **Fits:** APIs, infrastructure, document automation, transcription, variable-volume AI — when the unit is understandable and tracks both value and cost.
- **Fails:** invisible internal units (tokens) for a buyer who thinks in documents; uncapped surprise bills; caps without cost controls (surprise losses); helping customers use less cuts your revenue.
- **Example:** 2,000 accepted documents × $0.08 = $160; at $0.03 per accepted document for processing, retries and review, $100 contribution.
- **First test:** run a shadow invoice on a real workload and ask the customer to predict it first. The gap shows whether the unit is clear.

## 4. Credits / consumables

- **Fits:** image, video and document generation; occasional processing; buyers who want controlled spending without a commitment.
- **Fails:** confusing exchange rates; stranded tiny balances; a model price rise making old credit promises expensive.
- **Example:** $30 buys 100 standard jobs; at $0.12 per redeemed job, full redemption costs $12, leaving $18 before fees and support. Assume full redemption.
- **First test:** put the credit balance next to three common jobs and ask the customer to convert it back into jobs. Pilot a small pack and watch for a repeat purchase.

## 5. Paid content / add-ons

- **Fits:** creative tools, learning, games, specialist modules — when different customers need genuinely different extensions.
- **Fails:** essential steps split into surprise charges; too many tiny modules; bundles hiding weak standalone value.
- **Example:** 1,000 active customers × 8% buying a $25 pack = $2,000, before production, licensing and support. The 8% is an assumption.
- **First test:** show the base outcome first, then one distinct upgrade outcome, to existing customers, before building a catalogue.

## 6. Transaction / commission

- **Fits:** booking platforms, service and commerce marketplaces, payments-enabled vertical software that improves discovery, trust or payment.
- **Fails:** two empty sides; treating transaction volume as revenue; a high take rate inviting people to meet and leave; refunds and support eating a thin fee.
- **Example:** $50,000 transaction value × 8% = $4,000 fees; plan on what's left after payment costs, refunds and support (say $2,500).
- **First test:** draw one funnel per side, then complete ten matches by hand and record why each worked or failed.

## 7. Ads / sponsorship

- **Fits:** media, discovery tools, newsletters, high-frequency consumer apps. A small specialist audience can support direct sponsorship when display ads wouldn't.
- **Fails:** downloads without repeat use; more ads lowering retention; cheap traffic worth less than it costs; one sponsor dominating revenue.
- **Example:** 100,000 impressions at $4 per thousand = $400; or two $500 sponsorships = $1,000 minus the selling time.
- **First test:** compare the audience needed for $1,000 of display ads with one specialist sponsorship; test a small placement and watch the core task.

## 8. Affiliate / lead generation

- **Fits:** comparison tools, eligibility checkers, calculators, matching — where the user is choosing an external provider.
- **Fails:** recommending the highest commission over the best fit; one partner changing terms; confusing this with paying people to refer users to your own product (that's distribution).
- **Example:** 1,000 relevant outbound visits × 3% approved × $40 = $1,200, before content and acquisition; reversals reduce it.
- **First test:** map result → recommendation → qualified action → approved payout, and validate one partner before building a database.

## 9. Enterprise contracts

- **Fits:** team workflows, analytics, infrastructure, business-critical systems bought by organisations. A focused indie product can serve them if scope and support stay manageable.
- **Fails:** long sales cycles exhausting a solo founder; bespoke commitments before revenue; revenue concentrated in a few accounts.
- **Example:** $24,000/yr − $5,000 hosting and support − $4,000 onboarding = $15,000 before sales cost — and count the founder's time to win it.
- **First test:** map the four-person buying committee (user, champion, budget owner, approver) before designing a checkout; offer a paid pilot with acceptance criteria and a decision date.

## 10. White-label / OEM

- **Fits:** agency platforms, embedded reporting, workflow engines that partners can sell to their own customers.
- **Fails:** one partner dominating revenue and roadmap; a separate codebase per partner; "fewer customers" that means many downstream users to support.
- **Example:** five partners × $600/mo = $3,000; losing one removes 20%.
- **First test:** demo the same product under two partner brands; pilot with one partner on a fixed integration scope before promising exclusivity.

## 11. Open core / hosting

- **Fits:** developer tools and infrastructure whose users value inspecting or self-hosting; buyers who pay to avoid running it.
- **Fails:** reading stars and downloads as paid demand; free community support that costs real time; licence terms not understood.
- **Example:** 50 hosted accounts × $30/mo = $1,500 before infrastructure, support and maintenance.
- **First test:** ask why someone who *can* self-host would pay — and answer it for this product.

## 12. Software + services

- **Fits:** automation, migration, reporting and vertical workflows; consultants and service owners who already know a recurring client problem — the natural model for a member productizing their skill.
- **Fails:** calling labour "software revenue" and hiding the capacity limit; unlimited support and loose revisions; founder hours left uncosted.
- **Example:** a $1,500 setup taking 12 hours at a $60 planning rate plus $80 tools leaves $700; a separate $99/mo software fee must cover its own costs.
- **First test:** turn one engagement into a repeatable package, record every step, and mark which steps could become product features.

## 13. Outcome-based

- **Fits:** repeatable, observable results the seller largely controls — resolved tickets, verified processing, agreed qualification events.
- **Fails:** vague "pay for growth" promises; results that depend on factors outside the product; easy proxies (booked meetings that aren't buyers).
- **Example:** 200 attempts × $0.30 produce 120 accepted outcomes × $1.50 = $180 revenue, $60 cost — cost per outcome is $0.50, not $0.30.
- **First test:** write down a billable event and a non-billable near miss; score a sample independently with the customer.

## 14. Donations / patronage

- **Fits:** community utilities, public-interest projects, open-source maintenance, tools a committed niche relies on.
- **Fails:** treating goodwill as a revenue floor; supporter perks turning into an accidental service business; large sponsors pulling against the audience.
- **Example:** 80 supporters × $5/mo = $400 — compare it with maintenance hours, and model the largest sponsors leaving.
- **First test:** write one concrete ask ("fund maintenance of this tool for this community") and check users understand what it changes.

## 15. Supports another business

- **Fits:** calculators, diagnostics, planning tools, portals, free skills and templates that feed a paid service or product the member already sells.
- **Fails:** a topic so broad it attracts unqualified users; a form disguised as a tool; usage costs above the business value it creates.
- **Example:** a free diagnostic produces 20 qualified enquiries, 3 become clients at $600 contribution each = $1,800 — minus those who'd have bought anyway.
- **First test:** map free result → the next problem → the paid offer, and interview users who use the tool but never need the paid offer.

## 16. Lifetime offers

- **Fits:** low-running-cost utilities and carefully capped software; early cash and engaged users. Weak for compute-heavy services.
- **Fails:** launch cash hiding a growing obligation; deal-hunting audiences unlike subscribers; heavy support; counting on the next launch to fund the last cohort.
- **Example:** $199 − $40 channel fees − $25 onboarding − $72 service cost over a six-year stress test = $62 before overhead. Six years is a test horizon, not permission to end the promise.
- **First test:** run low, expected and heavy-use cost scenarios; cap the first offer by a delivery budget.
