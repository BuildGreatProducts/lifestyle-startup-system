# Offer failure patterns

Eight patterns that explain why most first-draft offers underperform. Name the pattern the moment a draft drifts into one ("that's a Wide Wedge") — a name gives the member a way to catch it next time — and propose the fix in the same breath. Each has a diagnostic question, a bad version, why it breaks, and a good version with its source in [offer-examples.md](offer-examples.md). The pricing patterns live in [pricing-models.md](pricing-models.md#pricing-failure-patterns).

## What good looks like, per element

| Element | Good | Bad |
|---|---|---|
| Customer | Specific, searchable, high value: a role, a stage or size, and a tool they've already tried | Vague, broad, a category |
| Pain | Specific, urgent, costly — reproducible in a screenshot or a one-paragraph story, in their words | A feeling, mild, hypothetical |
| Outcome | Tangible and measurable — the buyer can tell within 30 days, with a number | A benefit ("better productivity") |
| Mechanism | Unique, based on experience, knowledge or data the member has; survives "could one ChatGPT prompt do this?" | Untested, unclear, buzzwords |
| Guarantee | Bold, tied to the outcome, costs the seller something if it fails | Low commitment, detached from the outcome |
| Proof | Testimonials, case studies, reviews, results — verifiable by a stranger in under five minutes | "Customers love it" |

The pitch the six assemble into: *"For [customer] to help them avoid [pain] and achieve [outcome]. [Product] does this using [mechanism], backed by [proof] and de-risking it with [guarantee]."*

## 1. The Wide Wedge

The customer line names a category, not a recognisable person.

**Diagnostic:** Who would *not* buy this? If you can't name an excluded group, the wedge is too wide.

**Bad:** "Small business owners who need better tools."

**Why it breaks:** Ads reach people who never convert; onboarding has to be generic; refunds climb because the wrong people signed up; the price has to flex across too many budgets and ends up wrong for all of them. Bench's early positioning had this — broad enough to feel inclusive, narrow enough that nobody felt addressed.

**Good (Linear):** "Engineering and product teams at fast-growing software startups (10–500 people) who feel slowed down by Jira's configuration sprawl but need more workflow rigor than Trello or Notion." A role, a stage, a size range, and two tools already tried.

**When productizing a skill:** "Anyone who needs [my skill]" is the usual Wide Wedge. Narrow to the client segment that already pays the member — the invoices name it.

## 2. The Vague Pain

The pain describes a feeling, not an event.

**Diagnostic:** Can the pain be reproduced in a screenshot or a one-paragraph story?

**Bad:** "Inefficient workflows that waste time and create frustration."

**Why it breaks:** Vague pain makes vague copy that gets ignored. Buyers act on a moment they remember; without one, the offer has nothing to fire on and campaigns never meet the buyer at the moment of intent.

**Good (Bricks Builder):** "WordPress freelancers shipping client sites that score under 60 on PageSpeed because Elementor and Divi stack shortcode soup that pushes load past 3 seconds." Run PageSpeed, get a number — measurable before and after.

**When productizing a skill:** The best pain lines are the member's own client intake calls. Ask what the client said, word for word, the week they got in touch.

## 3. The Unmeasurable Outcome

The outcome is a benefit, not a result.

**Diagnostic:** Thirty days in, could the buyer tell whether they got it?

**Bad:** "Better productivity and improved quality for your team."

**Why it breaks:** The buyer churns on vibes; refund requests become unwinnable arguments; the guarantee collapses because there's nothing concrete to guarantee.

**Good (Bricks Builder):** "…scores 95+ on PageSpeed Insights out of the box." Verifiable in 60 seconds; one number does the work of the whole line.

## 4. The Feature-List Mechanism

The mechanism is marketing chrome, not an explanation of how it works.

**Diagnostic:** Does it name one thing about how the product works that a competitor can't copy in a sprint?

**Bad:** "AI-powered, cloud-native platform with real-time collaboration and seamless integrations."

**Why it breaks:** Every competitor can claim it. The mechanism is where the offer earns the right to charge more than the cheapest credible alternative; in buzzwords, the price ceiling collapses with it.

**Good (Linear):** "A keyboard-first, opinionated workflow engine on a custom real-time sync layer. Sub-50ms loads… — the opinion is the product." A technical bet, a measurable consequence, a posture.

**When productizing a skill:** "My 12 years of experience" is a credential, not a mechanism. Name the method the experience produced — the checklist, the sequence, the data, the judgment calls — that the product runs every time. For AI products, see [ai-era-patterns.md](ai-era-patterns.md#mechanism-patterns-that-signal-real-moat).

## 5. The Toothless Guarantee

The guarantee costs the seller nothing if the outcome fails.

**Diagnostic:** Does it reduce buyer risk in a way the seller would feel?

**Bad:** "Satisfaction guaranteed — we're confident you'll love it."

**Why it breaks:** A guarantee is a risk transfer. If the seller bears no risk, the buyer gets no relief and reads past it; the objection it was meant to defuse stays alive.

**Good (Linear):** "Free for unlimited members on the Standard plan, no credit card, no time-limited trial. Cancel paid plans in one click and keep your read-only data." It costs Linear real money, and it removes trial pressure, card lock-in and data hostage in one line.

## 6. The Hypothetical Proof

The proof names belief instead of an artifact.

**Diagnostic:** Can you point to a public artifact — a review count, a named customer, a revenue screenshot, a podcast mention?

**Bad:** "Customers love the product and tell us it transforms their workflow."

**Why it breaks:** Skeptical buyers discount anything a seller says about itself. Without external artifacts the offer converts only people who were already going to buy.

**Good (Designjoy):** "$1.5M+ ARR run solo, with monthly Stripe screenshots posted publicly… client work from Castmagic, Tally, OpenPhone." Every claim checkable in under five minutes.

**When productizing a skill:** A new product has no reviews, but the member has a track record. Results delivered by hand count: named clients (with permission), before/after numbers, testimonials, a portfolio. Use those until the product has its own.

## 7. The Story Break

Each line reads fine alone; together they describe three products.

**Diagnostic:** Read the assembled pitch out loud. One product, or a Mad Libs page?

**Bad:** Customer "Enterprise CIOs at Fortune 500 companies" · Outcome "Save individual designers two hours a week" · Proof "4.7 stars on the Mac App Store".

**Why it breaks:** The CIO doesn't care about designer hours and isn't moved by App Store stars. Each line came from a different draft and was never reconciled. Almost every weak offer fails here, and it's the cheapest fix — but only if someone reads the sentence aloud.

**Good (CleanShot X):** Mac power users, a 10-second capture, native Swift, a 14-day refund, 4.9 App Store stars and Wirecutter — one person, one buying context.

## 8. The Frame Mismatch

The offer is sold in one frame (consumer, B2B, enterprise, developer, indie) to a customer who lives in another.

**Diagnostic:** Imagine the named customer reading it cold. Do the trust signals, language and price feel native to how they buy?

**Bad:** Customer "CFOs at mid-market companies" · Proof "Featured on Product Hunt, trending on Hacker News" · Guarantee "Try it free for 14 days, cancel anytime".

**Why it breaks:** A CFO buys through procurement, security review, SOC2 and references; launch-site rankings and a consumer trial make the offer feel unserious. It's fatal and nearly invisible to a founder inside their own product.

**Good (Linear):** every line stays in the software team's frame — logos that team trusts, reviews on G2 where they shop, team-level free seats rather than a personal trial.

## How they compound

A Wide Wedge usually causes a Vague Pain (no specific person, so no specific pain), which causes an Unmeasurable Outcome, which causes a Toothless Guarantee. When several appear, trace them to the earliest one and fix that first — it often clears two or three below it.
